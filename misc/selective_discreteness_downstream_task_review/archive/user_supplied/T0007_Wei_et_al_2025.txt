Abstract— Network signal control is an effective way to mit-
igate traffic congestion. However, most network signal control
methods ignore the risk of queue spillback. Although local and
decentralized control methods have the potential to address
spillback, their performance at the system/network level is not
guaranteed, making it challenging to achieve the global optimum.
This study proposes a novel hierarchical model predictive control
(MPC) approach that utilizes the link transmission model (LTM)
with queue transmission at both road segment and turn levels
to optimize traffic signals for road networks. At the network
level, the controller employs a state-of-the-art LTM framework
that can describe segment-level flow dynamics and uses quadratic
programming to determine the effective fractions of green time
for network throughput maximization. The network decisions of
green time fractions are sent to the local layer as a reference.
The local layer builds on a refined LTM with turn-level queue
transmission and formulates a nonlinear programming problem
to track the reference to ensure that the optimal network decision
is realized at the individual intersections while eliminating
potential spillback. Simulation experiments on both an arterial
and a grid network are conducted to verify the performance
of the proposed approach. The results reveal that the proposed
MPC leads to substantial improvements in terms of throughput
and queue length, especially in oversaturated conditions, and is
robust against demand prediction errors.
Index Terms— Model predictive control, traffic signal con-
trol, link transmission model, macroscopic traffic flow, queue
spillback.

A. Related Work
Among the network-wide traffic control, researchers and
traffic engineers have explored various modeling and opti-
mization methods, which can be broadly divided into three
categories based on the control architecture: centralized, dis-
tributed, and hierarchical.
Centralized traffic control involves a control center that
manages intersections across an entire network or a sub-
stantial portion of it. Benefits of centralized control in
reducing congestion and externalities have been shown in
literature, e.g., perimeter control using macroscopic funda-
mental diagrams (MFDs) [1], [5], store-and-forward modeling
paradigm-based network-wide traffic signal control [6], [7],
[8] and its extension to multi-commodity traffic signal control
in the presence of connected vehicles [9]. However, cen-
tralized real-time traffic signal control is only applicable to
relatively small-scale networks due to the complexity of the
involved optimization problem. Moreover, large-scale road
networks using centralized control require a high-level infras-
tructure investment and maintenance, and and is vulnerable
to subsystem failures [10], [11]. Distributed control systems
allow individual intersections to make independent control
decisions based on the local conditions, e.g., max pressure
or back pressure-based methods [12], [13], [14], but these
methods face challenges in achieving network-wide optimiza-
tion because limited coordination can be achieved among
intersections. Hierarchical control offers a balance between
centralized control’s holistic view and coordination capabilities
and distributed control’s scalability, which has emerged as a
promising choice for improving large-scale network perfor-
mance. In this framework, the controlled systems are organized
into hierarchies, with lower-level controllers making decisions
for local intersections based on the local conditions and higher-
level controllers coordinating and optimizing network traffic
across intersections. The PRODYN [15] and SCATS [16]
are typical bi-level approaches that have been successfully

Unfortunately, they are not suitable for oversaturated traffic
conditions due to the rapidly changing flow operation and the
false estimation or prediction of flow parameters (e.g., arrival

Traffic flow models have been proven important in designing
and testing network signal control strategies, especially model-
based strategies such as model predictive control (MPC)
[8], [17], [18], [19], [20]. The MPC-based network control
methods usually used macroscopic traffic flow models to
formulate the network flow propagation, and many existing
studies rely on the MFDs [1], [17]. The MFDs-based meth-
ods assume that the traffic state within a (sub-)network is
homogeneous [21], [22], [23], [24], but this may not always
hold for congested traffic states, especially under oversaturated
conditions. Another group of studies used cell transmission
models (CTMs) that divide a road segment (RS) into some
spatial cells to formulate the flow dynamics, but the computa-
tional complexity caused by the sophisticated spatiotemporal
discretization poses challenges in terms of scalability and
real-time implementation in large networks [19], [25]. This
is particularly the case in oversaturated conditions where we
need to trace the queue development and spillback. Employing
CTMs to trace queue will entail fine discretization of road seg-
ments, resulting in excessive computational overheads (more
road cells involved in the optimization problem) [26], [27].
van de Weg et al. [19], [25] have used standard link
transmission models (LTMs) as the prediction model for
network traffic control. The standard LTM does not explicitly
model turn-level queue dynamics within the road. Instead,
it focuses on calculating aggregated inflows and outflows at the
upstream and downstream boundaries of each link. Although
the standard LTM can estimate vehicle accumulation within
a link over time, this is not equivalent to queue formation.
In congested conditions, accumulation includes both queued
vehicles and those still moving upstream of the queue. The
queue density typically varies between critical density and jam
density [27]. Therefore, the standard LTM, which assumes
only a constant jam density, is less capable of estimating
the varying queue density and queue length within the link.
Moreover, they treat traffic as a fluid flowing along the road,
without distinguishing between different turning movements
(e.g., left, right, or straight). Wei et al. [28] formulates a
link-based flow model with turn-level queue transmission to
simulate traffic flow propagation and queue dynamics. The
queue length is crucial for measuring signal control perfor-
mance [29]. However, their study only focused on model
development and verification.
With the development of machine learning, intelligent con-
trol methods have gained considerable attention in signal
control. Model-free deep learning/reinforcement learning-
based approaches are used to train the agent for signal
control scheme [30], [31], [32]. Zhou et al. [33] introduced
a novel distributed deep reinforcement learning method for
the network-wide signal control coordination, reducing com-
munication overhead while ensuring control collaboration in
reducing queue length. Ren et al. [34] introduces multi-agent
coordinated policy optimization (MACoPO), a coordination
algorithm based on multi-agent RL, for multi-intersection sig-
nal control. While these data-driven model-free signal control
methods have shown the potential to improve traffic efficiency
without the need for physical models, the interpretability of
the controller is limited and safety constraints are difficult to
address, raising concerns about trust and accountability in road
environments. There is still a need to develop advanced model-
based control methods.
B. Contributions
This study proposed a hierarchical MPC framework using
LTM with queue transmission (MPC-Q) to optimize traffic
signal for urban networks.The main contributions of this study
are summarized as follows:
Firstly, this study extends the general link-based MPC
framework to involve turn-level queue dynamics. Unlike exist-
ing link-based MPC methods that only consider aggregated
flow dynamics at the segment level, the proposed MPC incor-
porates flow dynamics and queue information at both the
segment and turn levels, enabling fine-grained signal control
optimization.
Secondly, compared to existing link-based MPC methods,
this study proposes a hierarchical control structure that allows
modular design of the two layers at different spatial-temporal
scales to adapt to real-time changes in turn-level queue lengths
at local intersections, while keeping the property to seek
system optimum over a long prediction horizon. This design
enables the addition of turn-level queue transmission with
computational tractability.
Specifically, an upper-layer network controller predicts net-
work flow propagation with segment-level aggregated flow
dynamics and determines the optimal total fraction of green
time for all turning movements of an approach. The objectives
of the network controller are to maximize the network through-
put and minimize the oscillations in the signal plan. The
optimal green time fraction of an approach is sent to a lower-
layer local intersection controller as the reference trajectory.
The local layer predicts the turn-level queue length dynamics
for each turning movement in an approach and determines the
turn-level optimal fraction of green time. The objective of the
local layer is to minimize the tracking error and queue length.
Experiments on the arterial and grid network are performed
to demonstrate the performance of the proposed approach.
The rest of the paper is organized as follows. Section II-A
provides the framework of the proposed MPC-Q. Then, Sec-
tions II-B and II-C formulate the proposed MPC-Q in detail,
in which the two-layer controller design is discussed explicitly.
Section III illustrates the numerical experiments in different
scenarios and compares our proposed method with the existing
methods to demonstrate the superiority of our method.

C. MPC-Q Controller Design
The extended LTM with queue transmission described in
the previous section consists of state equations and constraints.
This section details how these state equations and constraints
can be included in the MPC so that the network throughput
can be optimized while decreasing the queue lengths.
1) Network Layer: The primary objective of the network-
layer controller is to maximize the network throughput
while adhering to the flow dynamic equations outlined in
Section II-B. Maximizing the network throughput can be
realized by minimizing the total travel time spent [19], [25].
Existing methods usually take the independent green time at
each simulation step as a control decision to optimize the travel
time [19], [20], [25], which fails to guarantee the stability
of the control plan, resulting in possible large oscillations
in green time even over a short time interval. To address
such limitation, this study minimizes the total travel time
while considering the stability of the control plan, whereby
formulating a quadratic programming (QP) problem with a
quadratic objective function by involving the variations in
decision variables. The objective still can be easily solved by
any optimization solver, i.e., MATLAB, CPLEX.

IV. C ONCLUSION
This study proposed a novel MPC approach that uses the
LTM with queue transmission to optimize the traffic signals
in urban road networks. In the network layer, a QP-based
controller with inputs of the decision variations was formulated
to improve the network-wide throughput. By converting the
generally used time-step independent signal timing into signal
timing variations, the proposed MPC-Q framework is capable
of considering signal control stability. In the local layer,
an NLP-based controller with reference tracking was devel-
oped to track and refine the segment-level effective fractions of
green time output by the network layer to the turn level. Thus,
the proposed MPC-Q can be performed without assuming that
flow operations in different turning directions are homoge-
neous. Both arterial and network simulation experiments were
performed to demonstrate the performance of the proposed
algorithms. The obtained simulation results have demonstrated
the superiority of the proposed framework compared to the
existing rival methods.
The main challenge is how to incorporate the traffic vari-
ations in turn movements within the MPC, while ensuring
that the MPC has less computational overheads to be real-
time feasible. To this end, the LTM with turn-level link
inflow, queue inflow and link outflow was integrated into a
hierarchical MPC framework, which enables the controller
to capture the detailed flow propagation in multiple turning
directions within the road segment without imposing complex
computational overheads on the network-layer coordination,
since the computational overheads can be reduced by aggregat-
ing turn-level flow dynamics into segment levels at the network
layer. Moreover, in each road segment, the continuous turn-
level effective fractions of green time are used as controller
decision variables instead of widely used binary values, e.g.,
0 for green and 1 for red, which eliminates the need for
solving the cumbersome MILP problems and enables MPC to
generate fine-grained yet real-time feasible outputs. Compared
to the existing MPC-based methods using LTMs [19], [25], the
proposed MPC-Q method addresses the limitation of being
difficult to adapt to traffic variations at turn level in large
networks and accounts for the queue transmission.
Urban traffic involves multiple vehicle types, e.g., buses,
bicycles, and trams, but the proposed MPC-Q does not account
for the operational differences among them. Multimodal traffic
should be considered in the future. Given the diversity of
potential demand variations, the robust network-level MPC
for general demand errors using LTM with queue dynamics
requires further investigation. Besides, the impacts of sudden
incidents, e.g., traffic accidents or moving bottlenecks, on flow
propagation are not considered in the current MPC-Q method.
Further research should be carried out to refine the controller
design to deal with extreme congestion conditions.
