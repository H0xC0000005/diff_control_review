## Common philosophy and different starting questions

A and C share the method-first principle: start from an independently supported
physical omission, test the strongest reasonable aggregate alternative, and retain
additional state only if it is necessary for the control question. Both keep
single-ramp common rate control and all-stream TST initially. Neither fixes a
service penalty, adds a microscopic vehicle model or assumes a positive result.

**A starts with discharge dynamics:** what happens after traffic merges, and can
an ordinary density/equilibrium law represent acceleration, disturbance and
capacity recovery? It first compares a changed aggregate demand law, and only
then considers dynamic recovery/readiness information if needed.

**C starts with physical location and finite opportunity:** over what road
segment can ramp traffic merge, and how do remaining distance, available receiving
space and lane-end urgency affect where/when merging succeeds? It first compares
an explicit longitudinally resolved aggregate model of that genuine geometry.

Thus the distinction is their entry point into the physical explanation, not
opposing philosophies or an established ranking of total modeling changes.

## Semantic illustrations and evidence boundary

For A, think of ramp traffic entering while still accelerating and affecting
downstream discharge. The review question is whether a public aggregate demand
law captures the relevant effect, or whether another state variable is needed.
This is a physical motivation, not an established same-history/action witness.
The inspected Laval/Cassidy/Daganzo merge model represents bounded acceleration
and traffic-stream voids at a site without an acceleration lane, demonstrating
why that mechanism and finite geometry are separable.
[Primary merge paper, Section 2](https://bpb-us-e1.wpmucdn.com/sites.gatech.edu/dist/f/3571/files/2023/03/merges.pdf).

For C, think of the different opportunities faced by traffic near the entrance
and near the end of a finite merge lane. The review question is whether known
geometry plus matched spatial aggregate observations already describe the effect.
Equal whole-lane totals with different longitudinal positions would not establish
equality of the approved per-cell observations. Distance to the end is public
geometry, not itself hidden information. The lane-model source includes
distance-to-end and cooperative incentives; this is a formulation antecedent,
not validation of our proposed ramp task or its hiddenness.
[Primary lane-model paper, route/cooperation sections](https://repository.tudelft.nl/file/File_2c4359e7-5e5a-4651-964f-6f3610ab89ae).

In A, an extra scalar speed/recovery state might be inferred by the observed mean
speed/history. In C, finer aggregate cells might already reveal the relevant
distribution. Neither source supports a full hidden-information/useful-action/
gradient chain for the proposed task. They justify questions to screen.

## Cross-cutting structural limit and suggested planning stance

Changing mechanisms does not overturn deterministic complete-state sufficiency.
If the full initial state of the new model and all its inputs are known, its
future remains reconstructible in principle. A/C must therefore establish both
physical relevance and information not determined by the admitted aggregate
history, followed by useful common-action relevance. Added complexity alone
does not supply that result.

The user's mixed-search preference supports a brief comparative source/mechanism
screen of A and C before committing to deep formulation of either. Compare
physical support, credible aggregate rival, plausible observation ambiguity and
actual required changes. Do not automatically combine A+C: their mechanisms can
interact, but combining them first makes attribution and minimal-state claims
harder. Select a narrow line only when that screen identifies a defensible lead.
